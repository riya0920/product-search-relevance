Navigational queries are the ones a lexical index is built for: the
user typed a brand and a product noun, the tokens are literally in the
title, and there is nothing for semantics to add. 'Need' queries are the
opposite -- no product noun appears at all, so BM25 has nothing to match
and the dense leg carries the segment.

THE DEPLOYMENT CONSEQUENCE: head queries are mostly navigational AND
carry 59% of volume. A weighted hybrid that is tuned on aggregate NDCG
will be tuned by the tail, which is most of the QUERIES and little of the
TRAFFIC. The mature answer is fusion weights that vary by query type --
lexical-heavy for navigational, semantic-heavy for exploratory -- which
is a router this build does not have.

Both models are identical in every respect except which negatives
entered the contrastive loss: uniformly sampled irrelevant products, vs
products BM25 ranked in its top 30 that the judgments call irrelevant.

MEASURED DELTA: -0.0064 NDCG@10 overall -- NEGATIVE. Hard-negative
mining is the trick this spec calls the highest-signal-per-effort move
in retail search, and on this corpus it made the model WORSE. Reporting
that is the point of running the experiment rather than citing it.

Why it fails here, and it is a property of the corpus rather than of
the technique:

  1. THE NEGATIVES ARE TOO SCARCE AND TOO SPECIFIC. Mining found hard
     negatives for only 60 of 271 training queries. In this taxonomy
     most products are graded 1 or above for most queries -- every
     complement category is a 1 -- so BM25's top 30 contains very few
     genuine grade-0 items. The mined set is small and skewed toward
     the handful of queries where BM25 fails badly.

  2. WITH FEW NEGATIVES, HARD ONES OVERFIT. Replacing a broad sample
     of easy negatives with a narrow sample of hard ones shrinks the
     effective diversity of the loss. The model sharpens one boundary
     and loses the general one, which is why the damage is worst on
     'need' queries (-0.0249) -- the segment that most needs a broad
     sense of what is far away.

  3. A 600-PRODUCT CATALOGUE IS NOT WHERE THIS TECHNIQUE LIVES. Hard-
     negative mining pays when uniform negatives are trivially
     separable, which is a property of catalogues with millions of
     items and dense near-duplicates. At this size the uniform
     negatives are already informative.

  The honest conclusion is not 'hard negatives do not work'. It is that
  this corpus cannot test the claim, and the standard practitioner fix
  -- MIXING hard and random negatives rather than substituting, which
  is what production pipelines actually do -- is the experiment I would
  run next and did not build.

4. SUBSTITUTES ABOVE COMPLEMENTS
==============================================================================
                         frac correctly ordered
bm25                                     0.6767

hybrid RRF                               0.4694
hybrid weighted 0.6/0.4                  0.5806

For a purchase-intent query a SUBSTITUTE must outrank a COMPLEMENT.
Someone searching for running shoes wants a different running shoe, not
socks. Both are 'related', a co-occurrence-driven system conflates them,
and the graded labels are what make the distinction measurable at all.
(Queries whose top-10 contains no substitute/complement pair are excluded,
which is why this is reported separately rather than folded into NDCG.)

THE FINDING, AND IT IS THE SHARPEST ONE IN THIS PROJECT: rank the systems
by NDCG and by this metric and you get DIFFERENT ORDERS.

  system                        NDCG@10   sub>comp
  reranked                       0.8114     0.6319
  hybrid weighted 0.6/0.4        0.8065     0.5806
  hybrid RRF                     0.7967     0.4694
  dense (random negs)            0.7801     0.4713
  bm25                           0.7742     0.6767
  dense (hard negs)              0.7737     0.3878

Best NDCG: reranked. Best substitute ordering: bm25.

They are not the same system, and BM25 -- the baseline everything else
beats on NDCG -- is at or near the top on this metric. The mechanism is
in the discount: NDCG@10 with 2^g-1 gains is dominated by whether the
Exact matches (gain 7) reach the first few slots. The relative order of
a Substitute (gain 3) and a Complement (gain 1) five slots down barely
moves the number. So a ranker can optimise NDCG happily while filling
its lower slots with accessories, and a shopper who wanted a different
pair of shoes gets shown socks.

This is the concrete form of 'aggregates hide everything in search'.
The fix is not a better retrieval leg -- it is either a listwise
objective that sees the grade ordering, or an explicit intent-aware
constraint in the ranking layer. Neither is built here; what IS built
is the measurement that says you need one.

Re-ranker adds 224.25 ms at p95 and +0.0147 NDCG@10.
NDCG PER MILLISECOND: +0.00007

Search is a latency-budgeted business, so the reranker's value is a
RATIO, not a delta. At a 40 ms rerank budget this fits; the number that
decides deployment is NDCG-per-ms against whatever else could spend those
milliseconds -- a bigger candidate set, a second retrieval leg, or
personalisation.

HONEST CAVEAT ON THESE TIMINGS: the index is 600 products in memory in a
single Python process. BM25 here scores the whole catalogue linearly.
These are relative costs between legs, not service latencies, and the
ranking of the legs is the only thing that transfers.

6. ZERO-RESULT RESCUE
==============================================================================
150 test queries with an unmatchable token appended:
  zero-result rate, strict conjunctive matching : 100.0%
  zero-result rate, after relaxation + fallback : 0.0%
  NDCG@10 of the rescued results                : 0.9000

A zero-result page is a lost session with a measurable price. The rescue
ladder is deliberately ordered: relax to OR first (cheap, stays lexical),
then fall back to semantic (expensive, can drift off-intent). Showing
something is not automatically better than showing nothing -- the NDCG of
the rescued set is reported so the tradeoff is visible rather than
assumed. Spelling correction, which is the other half of a real rescue
ladder, is not implemented.
